Linear is a product-development platform for software teams and the AI agents that work with them. It combines issue tracking, project planning, product requests, analytics, integrations, and agent workflows in one structured system.

The company is an interesting SaaS case because it has moved from a focused developer tool toward a broader system for agent-driven product development while keeping a relatively simple monetization model: recurring subscriptions priced mainly by team size and feature level.

## Product and Industry Context

Linear operates in the project management, issue tracking, and product-development software market. The market includes several overlapping categories:

- **Engineering issue tracking:** work items, bugs, pull requests, releases, and development cycles.
- **Product and portfolio planning:** projects, roadmaps, initiatives, dependencies, and cross-team reporting.
- **Work intake and customer feedback:** requests from Slack, email, support, and other channels.
- **AI-assisted execution:** triage, summarization, coding agents, analytics, and automated workflows.

The industry is becoming more competitive because established platforms are adding AI rather than leaving the category to new AI-native products. Linear therefore competes on more than a checklist of project-management features. Its differentiation is the combination of a fast, opinionated interface, structured product context, developer integrations, and agent participation in the same workflow.

## What Linear Offers

Linear's core objects are issues, projects, and initiatives. Teams can organize work into cycles, track progress on timelines, connect work to code changes, and use integrations with development and communication tools.

Recent product positioning emphasizes a shared environment where people and agents plan, build, and coordinate together. Linear's public growth update states that agents are installed in 95% of paid workspaces and that 50% of newly created work is now attributed to agents. These are company-reported product metrics, not independent market measurements.

The product strategy has two effects:

1. It increases the amount of work managed inside Linear.
2. It gives the company more opportunities to charge for advanced automation, AI features, and enterprise controls.

## Business Model

Linear uses a product-led subscription SaaS model. Teams can start without a lengthy implementation project, then expand as they add users, projects, teams, and enterprise requirements.

### Freemium entry point

The Free plan supports unlimited members, two teams, 250 issues, the agent platform, and Linear Agent. It lowers the cost of trying the product and creates a path for bottom-up adoption inside a company.

### Per-seat subscriptions

The Basic plan is listed at $10 per user per month, and the Business plan at $16 per user per month. This creates predictable recurring revenue and naturally expands when a product team grows or when more departments adopt the platform.

### Enterprise contracts

Enterprise pricing is custom and includes capabilities such as SAML, SCIM, granular administration, security controls, onboarding, priority support, and account management. Enterprise contracts can increase average revenue per account and make Linear relevant to larger organizations, although they also create higher support and sales costs.

### AI as an expansion layer

AI features are included in higher tiers or require AI credits, depending on the feature. Linear's pricing page lists Triage Intelligence, Loops, Code Intelligence, Linear Insights, Linear Asks, and related integrations under Business. AI can therefore serve two purposes: differentiating the product and increasing the value of higher-priced plans.

The current public pricing page does not provide enough information to calculate the percentage of revenue generated specifically by AI. It is more accurate to describe AI as a feature and expansion mechanism inside the subscription model than as a separately disclosed revenue line.

## Competition

Linear's competitors fall into three groups.

| Group | Examples | Competitive pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Established work-management platforms | Jira, Asana, ClickUp, and Monday.com | These products offer broad workflows, large ecosystems, and enterprise purchasing familiarity. Their breadth can be an advantage for cross-functional organizations, while Linear emphasizes a more focused product-development workflow. |
| Developer and product tools | GitHub Projects, GitLab planning features, and Shortcut | These tools benefit from proximity to source control or software delivery. Linear must justify becoming the team's central planning layer rather than relying on tools already included in a development stack. |
| AI-native and open-source challengers | Plane and other agent-oriented project tools | Plane positions itself as a project-management and knowledge workspace with AI agents, integrations, and cloud, self-hosted, and air-gapped deployment options. Self-hosting is a meaningful differentiator for organizations with data-control requirements. |

Jira is the most important incumbent comparison. Atlassian's current Jira offering includes free, Standard, Premium, and Enterprise tiers, along with automation, planning, AI features, integrations, and enterprise security. Linear's opportunity is to win teams that value a smaller, faster-feeling product and are willing to trade some breadth or ecosystem maturity for a more opinionated workflow.

Plane represents a different threat. Its public product pages emphasize AI agents, built-in documentation, migration from Linear and Jira, self-hosting, and air-gapped deployment. That gives Plane a credible position with organizations that want open deployment or control over infrastructure.

## Revenue Composition

Linear does not publish a revenue-by-product or revenue-by-segment breakdown. A defensible analytical model is therefore based on the pricing and packaging structure:

| Revenue component | Likely mechanism | Public support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic subscriptions | Per-user recurring subscriptions at the published Basic rate | Directly listed on Linear's pricing page. |
| Business subscriptions | Per-user recurring subscriptions with AI, analytics, intake, and integration features | Directly listed on Linear's pricing page. |
| Enterprise subscriptions | Custom annual contracts with security, administration, support, and onboarding | Directly listed on Linear's pricing page. The actual contract values are not public. |
| Expansion revenue | More seats, more teams, upgrades, and broader organizational adoption | Consistent with the per-seat model and Linear's reported 177% net revenue retention. Exact contribution is not disclosed. |
| AI-related value | Higher-tier features and AI credits within paid plans | The pricing page confirms AI features and credits, but not a separate AI revenue percentage. |

The likely revenue hierarchy is subscription revenue first, enterprise expansion second, and AI as a value and retention layer within those plans. It would be speculative to claim that Linear earns a specific percentage from AI, consulting, or professional services.

At more than $100M ARR and more than 40,000 paying companies, a simple average would be roughly $2,500 of ARR per paying organization. This is only an arithmetic average, not Linear's actual ARPA: enterprise accounts may be much larger than small teams, and the customer count may include accounts with different definitions or billing arrangements.

## Operating Cost Structure

Linear does not publish COGS, operating expenses, or an income statement. The cost structure can nevertheless be organized into the following categories.

### Cost of goods sold

- cloud hosting, databases, and the synchronization layer;
- storage, bandwidth, backups, and observability;
- third-party integrations and email or notification delivery;
- AI model inference and agent execution;
- customer support directly associated with service delivery.

AI introduces a variable cost that traditional issue tracking did not have. The effect on gross margin depends on model selection, request volume, context size, caching, and whether AI work is included in a plan or metered with credits.

### Operating expenses

- engineering, product, design, and security compensation;
- sales, customer success, and enterprise account management;
- marketing, community, and developer relations;
- legal, finance, recruiting, compliance, and administration;
- office, travel, insurance, and other corporate costs.

A high ARR per employee can indicate strong operating leverage, but it does not establish profit. Compensation, stock-based compensation, infrastructure commitments, and growth investment may still be substantial.

## Illustrative Profit Model

Because Linear has not published its income statement, the following is an analytical scenario rather than a report of actual profit. It uses the reported $100M ARR as a revenue proxy and explicit assumptions.

| Base-case item | Assumption | Annual amount |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Revenue proxy | $100M ARR treated as annual recurring revenue | $100.0M |
| COGS | 18% of revenue for infrastructure, AI, support, and delivery | -$18.0M |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus COGS | $82.0M |
| Research and development | 34% of revenue | -$34.0M |
| Sales, marketing, and customer success | 14% of revenue | -$14.0M |
| General and administrative | 10% of revenue | -$10.0M |
| Illustrative operating profit | Before taxes and exceptional items | $24.0M |

Under this scenario:

- gross margin is 82%;
- operating margin is 24%;
- COGS is $18M;
- total operating expenses are $58M;
- operating profit is $24M.

These values should not be described as Linear's actual COGS or net income. They illustrate one plausible cost profile for a mature software company with meaningful AI usage, enterprise support, and a large product team.

### Sensitivity analysis

| Scenario | COGS | Operating expenses | Illustrative operating profit |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Efficient | 12% ($12M) | 48% ($48M) | $40M |
| Base | 18% ($18M) | 58% ($58M) | $24M |
| Investment-heavy | 28% ($28M) | 75% ($75M) | -$3M |

The sensitivity range is wide because the public ARR figure does not reveal the company's hiring pace, AI consumption, compensation structure, stock-based compensation, or sales investment. Linear's statement that it is cash-flow positive is stronger evidence than a speculative margin estimate, but cash flow and accounting profit are different measures.

## Profit Composition

The likely sources and uses of profit can be described as follows:

- **Subscription gross profit:** recurring seat-based revenue minus the incremental cost of hosting, synchronization, support, and integrations.
- **Enterprise gross profit:** larger contracts can produce more revenue per account, but they also require onboarding, security reviews, account management, and support.
- **AI contribution:** AI may increase willingness to pay and retention, but inference costs can reduce gross margin when usage is high. The contribution depends on pricing and controls that are not publicly disclosed.
- **Expansion profit:** upgrades and additional seats can be particularly attractive because the product and infrastructure are already deployed, although more users also increase support and usage costs.

The largest operating expense is likely people. Linear's official About page lists a distributed team across North America and Europe and more than 30 open positions across engineering, product, design, sales, support, and other functions. The exact headcount and compensation mix are not stated, so any precise profit-per-employee calculation would be unreliable.

## Financing and Team Scale

Linear was founded in 2019. Sacra reports the following financing history:

| Round | Reported amount | Reported timing |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Seed | $4.2M | November 2019 |
| Series A | $13M | December 2020 |
| Series B | $35M | September 2023 |
| Series C | $82M | June 2025 |
| Total primary funding | $134.2M | Sacra estimate and compilation |

In August 2026, Linear announced a $99M tender offer at a $2.5B valuation. The company explicitly described this as a way for current and former employees to obtain liquidity, not as a new primary capital raise. Linear also stated that it was cash-flow positive and held more cash than it had raised in primary funding.

This financing history suggests a company that used venture capital to build the product and scale, but is no longer raising capital simply to fund ordinary operations. Its current strategy appears to combine financial independence with selective hiring and continued product expansion.

## Conclusion

Linear's public profile supports a clear but limited conclusion:

- it has surpassed $100M in ARR according to the company's August 2026 announcement;
- more than 40,000 companies pay for the product;
- the company reports 177% net revenue retention and positive cash flow;
- public pricing combines free adoption, per-seat subscriptions, Business features, and custom Enterprise contracts;
- Sacra reports approximately $134.2M of primary funding;
- the exact current team size, COGS, net profit, revenue mix, and profit mix are not publicly disclosed.

The business model is powerful because the same product can expand from a small engineering team to an enterprise organization, while AI agents increase the amount of work flowing through the platform. The main financial risks are also clear: infrastructure and AI costs may rise with usage, enterprise support is expensive, and continued hiring can absorb a large share of gross profit.

Linear's economics should therefore be evaluated using both growth and discipline. ARR and net retention show the strength of the subscription engine; cash flow indicates that the company is not dependent on constant fundraising; and the missing income-statement details define the remaining uncertainty around true net profitability.
