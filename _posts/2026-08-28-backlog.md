# Backlog: A Practical Guide

A backlog is an ordered list of work that may be needed to achieve a product or project goal. It can contain feature requests, defects, technical improvements, research tasks, and other work items.

The important word is **ordered**. A backlog is not just a storage area for ideas. Its order communicates what matters most now, while lower items can remain less detailed until they move closer to implementation.

## Product Backlog and Sprint Backlog

Scrum defines two related artifacts:

### Product Backlog

The Product Backlog is the evolving list of work for a product. The Product Owner is accountable for:

- Communicating the Product Goal
- Creating and explaining backlog items
- Ordering the items
- Keeping the backlog transparent and understood

The Product Backlog is not a contract that fixes the entire future scope. It changes as the team and stakeholders learn more about the product.

### Sprint Backlog

The Sprint Backlog is the plan for the current Sprint. It combines:

- The Sprint Goal
- The Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint
- The Developers' plan for delivering them

The Developers select the work with the Product Owner and adapt their plan as they learn more during the Sprint.

## What Makes a Backlog Useful?

A useful backlog gives the team a shared view of upcoming work without pretending that every detail is already known. Good backlog items usually make clear:

- The problem or opportunity
- The expected value
- The conditions for considering the work complete
- Important dependencies or constraints

Items near the top should be sufficiently clear for near-term planning. Items further down can remain rough hypotheses rather than receiving premature design and estimation effort.

## Common Backlog Items

- New product capabilities
- User stories
- Bug fixes
- Performance or reliability improvements
- Security and maintenance work
- Technical debt
- Research and exploration

Keeping these categories in one ordered view makes trade-offs visible. For example, a feature request competing with a reliability fix is a prioritization decision, not merely a scheduling detail.

## A Simple Maintenance Routine

Backlog management is an ongoing activity:

1. Remove items that no longer support the goal.
2. Reorder the remaining items when value, risk, or information changes.
3. Clarify the next items before planning begins.
4. Break down selected items only as much as the team needs to create a usable increment.
5. Review the result and update the backlog based on what was learned.

This keeps the backlog connected to current decisions. It also prevents two common failures: treating the backlog as an unfiltered list of wishes, and spending too much time specifying work that may never be selected.
