[w3schools.com](https://www.w3schools.com/) and its unrelated Chinese-language namesake [w3school.com.cn](https://www.w3school.com.cn/) are both still around after two-plus decades of "HTML/CSS/JS tutorial" content that barely changes year to year. That's not an accident — it's a specific, low-effort business model.

## 1. Content cost is nearly fixed

W3Schools was created in 1998 and is owned and run by [Refsnes Data AS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W3Schools), a small Norwegian company — not a large editorial organization. The core subjects (HTML, CSS, JavaScript, SQL) evolve slowly, so a tutorial page written years ago mostly still holds up. Once a topic page exists, it needs occasional edits, not a rewrite — the marginal cost of keeping it online approaches zero.

## 2. SEO traffic compounds for years

Per its own [about page](https://www.w3schools.com/about/default.asp), W3Schools reports:

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Years in business | 25+ |
| Monthly visitors | 70M+ |
| Pages displayed yearly | 3BN+ |

Search terms like "html tutorial" or "css reference" have huge, stable, long-tail search volume, and pages that already rank well tend to keep ranking — Google/search-engine trust accumulates rather than resets. Even a low per-page ad rate turns into real revenue at that scale.

## 3. Ads don't have to be prominent to pay

The free tutorial pages carry ads, and the site sells a [Plus subscription](https://order.w3schools.com/plans) specifically to remove them — implying ads are a real, ongoing revenue line worth paying to opt out of, even though they occupy a small footprint on the page (a banner, a sidebar slot). Billed at CPM/CPC, that adds up across tens of millions of monthly visits regardless of how visually unobtrusive it looks.

## 4. Paid add-ons stack on top of free content

Beyond ads, W3Schools sells several extra products from the same free traffic base:

- **Certifications** — individual exams (HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Python, SQL, etc.) at $95 each, up to a discounted "Full Access" bundle, per the [certification catalog](https://campus.w3schools.com/collections/certifications).
- **Spaces** — a subscription, browser-based code editor/hosting product ("2M+ Spaces Created" per the [Spaces page](https://www.w3schools.com/spaces/)) that upsells the same visitors who came for free tutorials.
- **Academy** — a for-teachers/schools plan advertised alongside Plus and Spaces.

None of this requires new traffic — it converts a slice of existing visitors at higher margin than display ads alone.

## 5. Small team, low overhead

Ownership by a small private company (Refsnes Data, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W3Schools)) rather than a large publisher means no large newsroom-style headcount or heavy infrastructure cost to support. With content cost near zero and traffic effectively free (organic search), the site breaks even easily and the rest is margin.

## 6. First-mover moat

Two decades of inbound links, brand recall, and top search rankings make it hard for a new competitor to out-rank W3Schools on the same generic keywords — the accumulated authority is itself a barrier to entry that a new site would need years to replicate.

## A parallel case: w3school.com.cn

[w3school.com.cn](https://www.w3school.com.cn/) runs the same playbook for Chinese-speaking learners: near-identical tutorial structure, long-tail keyword coverage in a market where "HTML教程"/"CSS教程" also has enduring search volume. I wasn't able to verify further operational details about that site directly (its pages timed out on fetch during research for this post), but the pattern — evergreen content, SEO-driven traffic, low update cadence — is the same one that keeps w3schools.com profitable.
